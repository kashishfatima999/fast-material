Week 5 — Democracy in Pakistan
Concept of Democracy · Historical Development · Structural Problems
▼

What is Democracy?

Democracy means rule of the people (Greek: demos = people, kratos = rule). Sovereignty belongs to citizens and authority is exercised through elected representatives.

Core Principles

  • Popular Sovereignty — People are the ultimate source of authority
  • Rule of Law — Law applies equally to all, from PM to citizen
  • Constitutional Supremacy — No institution is above the Constitution
  • Separation of Powers — Executive, Legislature, and Judiciary are independent
  • Political Pluralism — Opposition parties are allowed and respected
  • Accountability & Transparency — Government decisions can be questioned

Pakistan operates under the 1973 Constitution establishing a parliamentary system.

Types of Democracy

  • Direct Democracy — Citizens vote directly on laws (e.g. Swiss referendums; not feasible in Pakistan)
  • Representative Democracy — Citizens elect representatives (National Assembly, Senate, Provincial Assemblies)
  • Social Democracy — Economic justice alongside political rights (e.g. Ehsaas Program)

Historical Timeline

1947–1958 Early Years: Refugee crisis, Kashmir War, leadership vacuum after Jinnah's death (1948). Six PMs in seven years. 1956 Constitution adopted, then abrogated by Iskander Mirza → Ayub Khan's Martial Law.

1958–1969 Ayub Khan: 1962 Presidential Constitution, Basic Democracies, economic growth but political repression, East Pakistan marginalized.

1971 Yahya Khan's 1970 elections: Mujib won, power not transferred → Civil war → Bangladesh.

1971–1977 Bhutto Era: 1973 Constitution (parliamentary, federalism, fundamental rights), but power centralization, opposition suppression, 1977 coup.

1977–1988 Zia-ul-Haq: Martial Law, Islamization, 8th Amendment (President could dismiss PM), democracy structurally weakened.

1988–1999 Decade of Democracy: Benazir (2 tenures), Nawaz (2 tenures), all four govts dismissed. Article 58(2)(b) enabled President to dismiss PM. Democracy functioned electorally but not institutionally.

1999–2008 Musharraf: 17th Amendment, judicial crisis 2007, Lawyers' Movement. 2008 elections restored democracy.

2008–2013 PPP (Zardari): 18th Amendment — parliamentary supremacy restored, provincial autonomy, presidential powers reduced. First party to complete full term.

2013–2018 PML-N (Nawaz): CPEC, energy improvements, Panama Papers → disqualification.

2018–2022 PTI (Imran Khan): welfare reforms, anti-corruption, IMF negotiations, removed via no-confidence vote.

2022–Present PDM then 2024 elections: Shehbaz Sharif PM, economic crisis, political polarization.

Structural Problems of Democracy in Pakistan

  • Military influence over civilian governments
  • Weak intra-party democracy
  • Judicial activism interfering with governance
  • Economic instability weakening governments
  • Elite dominance in politics
  • Political intolerance and polarization

Conclusion

Pakistan's democracy has collapsed three times (1958, 1977, 1999), survived constitutional crises, and returned through elections repeatedly. It remains resilient but fragile. Its future depends on constitutional supremacy, civilian control, economic reforms, and institutional balance.

Week 7 — Issues of Federalism & New Provinces
New Province Demands · Local Government · 18th Amendment
▼

Current Federal Structure

Pakistan has four provinces: Punjab, Sindh, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK), Balochistan; plus federal territories: Islamabad Capital Territory, Gilgit-Baltistan, and Azad Jammu & Kashmir.

Why Regions Demand New Provinces

  • Unequal Development — Large provinces concentrate spending in major cities (e.g. Lahore vs South Punjab)
  • Administrative Inefficiency — Punjab has 120+ million people; too large to manage
  • Cultural/Linguistic Identity — Seraiki speakers, Hazara people feel identity is ignored
  • Political Representation — Peripheral regions underrepresented in provincial leadership

Constitutional Framework — Article 239(4)

Creating a new province requires: (1) Constitutional amendment bill in Parliament, (2) Two-thirds majority in both National Assembly and Senate, (3) Two-thirds majority in the concerned Provincial Assembly.

Major Provincial Demands

  • South Punjab — Multan, Bahawalpur, DG Khan, Rahim Yar Khan. Higher poverty, lower literacy, political marginalization from Central Punjab.
  • Bahawalpur — Formerly a princely state; demand for restoration of its administrative identity.
  • Hazara Province — Abbottabad, Mansehra, Haripur; demand arose after 2010 KPK renaming emphasized Pashtun identity. Hazara people speak Hindko.
  • Seraiki Province — Linguistic identity basis; Seraiki speakers argue other provinces (Sindh, KPK) are language-based.
  • Urban Sindh (Karachi/Hyderabad) — Claims urban areas contribute most tax revenue but decisions controlled by rural Sindhi elites.
  • FATA Merger (2018) — FATA merged into KPK; abolished Frontier Crimes Regulation; shows reorganization is possible with political consensus.

Arguments For New Provinces

  • Better governance through smaller units
  • Balanced development and resource distribution
  • Political empowerment of local populations
  • Strengthening federalism

Arguments Against New Provinces

  • Ethnic fragmentation risk
  • High financial cost (new secretariats, courts, bureaucracies)
  • Political manipulation for votes
  • Administrative complexity in federal coordination

Administrative vs Ethnic Models

Administrative model — Based on governance efficiency (e.g. divide Punjab into North/Central/South). Reduces ethnic tensions.
Ethnic model — Based on cultural identity (e.g. Seraiki province). Strengthens identity but may intensify ethnic politics.

Local Government Systems

Basic Democracies (1959, Ayub Khan) — 4-tier structure; 80,000 Basic Democrats served as electoral college for President. Bureaucracy dominated; criticized as legitimizing authoritarian rule.

Local Govt Ordinance 1979 (Zia-ul-Haq) — Separate urban/rural structures; local taxation powers; but provinces retained suspension/dissolution authority.

Local Govt Ordinance 2001 (Musharraf) — Most ambitious reform; devolution of political, administrative, financial authority to districts. District Nazim as political head. Citizen Community Boards, Zila Mohtasib. Dismantled after 2007.

18th Amendment (2010) — Article 140A

Each province must establish local government, devolve political/administrative/financial authority, and hold local elections via Election Commission. First time LGs received constitutional protection. Provinces enacted own LG laws in 2013.

Provincial LG Comparison

  • Punjab — Restored urban-rural divide; District Education/Health Authorities under provincial control
  • Sindh — Union Committees replace Union Councils; Mayor supervisory but province retains control
  • KPK — Most decentralized; Village/Neighborhood Councils; 30% dev budget guaranteed to LGs
  • Balochistan — Strong provincial supervision; divisional coordination committees

Population per local unit: KPK 2,000–10,000 | Balochistan 1,000–1,500 | Punjab 10,000–20,000 | Sindh 40,000–50,000

Week 8 — Neo-Liberal Governance & Poverty
Neoliberalism · Privatization · Structural Adjustment · Inequality
▼

Colonial Roots of Poverty

British colonial rule extracted resources (cotton, wheat, minerals), weakened local industries, and forced cash crop farming. This is called accumulation by dispossession — wealth transferred from local populations to powerful groups. Pakistan inherited a weak industrial base and dependence on foreign aid.

Phases of Capitalism

  • Mercantilism (16th–18th c.) — Accumulate gold; colonize for resources
  • Industrial Capitalism (19th c.) — Factories, mass production, urbanization
  • Welfare Capitalism (Post-WWII) — Social security, public healthcare, free education
  • Neoliberal Capitalism (1970s+) — Free markets, privatization, limited state intervention

The 1970s economic crisis of overaccumulation (too much capital, not enough profitable investment) pushed companies to seek new markets in developing countries.

Accumulation by Dispossession (David Harvey)

Wealth created by taking public resources and transferring to private owners through: privatization, land dispossession, commodification of natural resources, contract labor, debt repayment burden, and regressive taxation (indirect taxes hitting the poor harder).

Marxist Analysis

Marx: capitalism divides society into bourgeoisie (capital owners) and proletariat (wage workers). Lenin: capitalism expands globally through imperialism — powerful countries dominate weaker ones economically.

Introduction of Neoliberalism in Pakistan

Adopted in late 1980s under pressure from IMF and World Bank. Programs included Stand-By Arrangements, Structural Adjustment Programs (SAP), and Poverty Reduction and Growth Facility. These required reducing subsidies, increasing taxes, and privatizing state enterprises.

Core Neoliberal Policies

1. Liberalization — Reducing government restrictions; lowering tariffs; allowing foreign companies. In Pakistan, cheap imports (especially from China) flooded the market, hurting local industries and causing unemployment.

2. Privatization — Transferring govt enterprises to private ownership. Examples: KESC privatized 2005 (electricity prices increased, service inconsistent); PTCL privatized 2006 (sold at low price, thousands lost jobs).

3. Deregulation — Removing government controls on businesses. Can lead to worker exploitation, environmental damage, monopolies.

Impact of Structural Adjustment Programs

  • Higher unemployment
  • Reduced welfare programs
  • Increased poverty
  • Cuts to education and healthcare spending

Social Restructuring

  • Elite class — Extremely wealthy, control industries, influence politics
  • Culture of Contentment — Middle-class professionals benefiting from globalization
  • Vulnerable workers — Contract/daily wage laborers with unstable jobs
  • Marginalized poor — Slum dwellers, unemployed youth excluded from economic opportunities

Human Insecurity Types

  • Job insecurity (temporary work, layoffs)
  • Health insecurity (expensive private hospitals)
  • Education insecurity (high private school fees)
  • Food insecurity
  • Environmental insecurity (industrial pollution)

Recent Poverty Drivers

Food inflation — Poor households spend 60–70% of income on food; price increases devastate them.
Energy prices — Pakistan imports oil; global price increases raise electricity, transport, and production costs.

Resistance & Alternatives

Lawyers' Movement protested privatization of Pakistan Steel Mills and political interference in judiciary. Alternatives to neoliberalism: strengthen welfare state, reform international financial institutions, design locally-suited development strategies.

Week 9 — Obstacles to Economic Development
Internal & External Barriers · Agriculture · Infrastructure · Governance
▼

What is Economic Development?

Continuous improvement in people's lives: better income, more jobs, improved education/healthcare, strong infrastructure, and poverty reduction.

Key Economic Problems

  • Inflation — Rising prices reduce purchasing power (e.g. flour Rs.50 → Rs.100)
  • Budget Deficit — Spending exceeds income → loans → increasing debt → less for development
  • Weak Governance — Poor policy implementation, corruption, declining public trust
  • Low Investment — Fewer factories, limited jobs, slow growth
  • Political Instability — Frequent govt changes → investor uncertainty → inconsistent policies

Historical Economic Overview

1947–1960 — No industrial base, weak banking, limited trained workforce. Built from scratch.
1960s — GDP growth 6.8%, industrialization, textile/cement/steel sectors. But growth under non-democratic rule; benefits unevenly distributed.
Post-1971 — Loss of East Pakistan weakened economy. Political instability, policy inconsistency.
1990s reforms — Privatization, trade liberalization, deregulation. Mixed results.

Agricultural Sector Problems

  • Water scarcity — Melting glaciers, poor irrigation, water wastage, unequal distribution
  • Poor management — Lack of farmer education, weak research, poor fertilizer distribution
  • Natural disasters — Reduce soil fertility, kill livestock, drive farmers into debt (2010 floods: billions in losses)
  • Traditional farming — Low yield, high water use, inefficient land use; modern technology could transform productivity

Internal Obstacles

  • SMEs — High interest rates, lack of technology, poor marketing; cannot compete globally
  • Labor market — Low literacy, lack of technical education, brain drain
  • Tax evasion — Complex tax system, low trust in government, weak enforcement
  • Regional inequality — Major cities (Karachi, Lahore, Islamabad) far better than rural areas; causes urban migration
  • Corruption & bureaucracy — Raises cost of doing business, discourages investors, slows decisions

External Obstacles

  • Political instability — Investors prefer stable countries
  • Terrorism — Increases risk, reduces investment
  • Foreign policy challenges — Tensions with India affect trade
  • Low FDI — Foreign investors want stability, security, profit certainty

Infrastructure Deficit

Poor roads, energy shortages, weak transport systems → high cost of production. Macroeconomic risks (inflation, debt, fiscal deficits) create further uncertainty.

Circular Economy

Traditional economy: Use → Dispose. Circular economy: Use → Reuse. Benefits: saves energy, reduces pollution, creates recycling jobs. Pakistan struggles due to lack of recycling plants, poor waste collection, low public awareness.

Slow Economic Diversification

Pakistan relies heavily on textile exports. If global textile demand falls, the entire economy becomes vulnerable. Banks prefer large businesses, leaving SMEs underdeveloped.

What Pakistan Needs

  • Strong, stable governance
  • Investment in education and skills
  • Better infrastructure
  • Support for industries and SMEs
  • Circular economy practices
Week 10 — Foreign Policy of Pakistan
Basic Concepts · National Interest · Determinants · Geo-Strategic Importance
▼

What is Foreign Policy?

Foreign policy = how a country deals with other countries; all decisions/plans/actions to protect national interests internationally. It covers political relations (treaties, embassies), economic relations (trade, investment), military relations (defense pacts), and cultural relations (soft power).

Key Features of Foreign Policy

  • Dynamic — Changes with global situations (e.g. Pakistan shifting from USA to China focus)
  • Interest-Based — Every decision protects national interest
  • Realistic/Practical — Countries act on benefit, not emotion

National Interest

Primary (Vital) Interests — Survival of state, border protection, national security/defense. A country will do anything to protect these.
Secondary Interests — Trade, economic growth, diplomatic relations, international reputation. These can change over time.

Key principle: "There are no permanent friends or enemies, only permanent interests."

Pakistan's Foreign Policy Goals

  • Protect sovereignty and territorial integrity
  • Build a strong economy
  • Develop friendly relations with all countries
  • Gain international recognition
  • Promote peace and cooperation

Phases of Pakistan's Foreign Policy

  • Early Years — Dependence on Western countries for aid and security
  • Cold War — Joined SEATO and CENTO for security against India and Soviet threat
  • Post-1971 — Focus on rebuilding after Bangladesh separation
  • Modern Era — Strong partnership with China (CPEC); balancing USA and China

Determinants of Foreign Policy

  • National Security — Most important; protection from India (external) and terrorism (internal)
  • Economic Factor — Strong economy = strong foreign policy; includes IMF loans, trade, CPEC
  • Ideological Factor — Pakistan's Islamic identity; promotes Muslim world unity while maintaining balance
  • Historical Factor — Past conflicts (India-Pakistan wars) create long-term mistrust
  • Geographical Factor — Connects South Asia, Central Asia, and Middle East; Gwadar Port as trade hub

Principles of Pakistan's Foreign Policy

  • Islamic unity — strengthen relations with Muslim countries
  • Good relations with neighbours
  • Peace — prefer dialogue over war
  • Self-determination — support for Kashmir, Palestine
  • Peaceful co-existence — respect sovereignty
  • UN charter and international law
  • Independent policy — balance major powers

Foreign Policy-Making Structure

Key actors: (1) Administrative Troika — PM, President, Army Chief; (2) Foreign Ministry — handles diplomacy; (3) Intelligence Agencies — security information; (4) Political Parties — public opinion; (5) Parliament — debate and accountability.

India Factor

India is the most important external factor since 1947 due to Kashmir dispute, past wars (1948, 1965, 1971), and military rivalry. Effects: heavy defense spending, need for alliances, continuous diplomatic efforts on Kashmir. Recent: India removed Article 370 (2019), Pulwama-Balakot crisis (2019).

Geo-Strategic Importance

Pakistan connects South Asia, Central Asia, and the Middle East. It borders China, India, Afghanistan, and Iran; has access to Arabian Sea. Gwadar Port can connect China and Central Asia to international markets via CPEC/Belt and Road Initiative.

Advantages — Trade opportunities, foreign investment, strategic value.
Disadvantages — Foreign interference, security threats, involvement in global conflicts.

Role in Global Conflicts

Cold War — Joined USA alliance (SEATO, CENTO) for security and economic aid; became dependent on USA.
War on Terror — Supported USA post-9/11, allowed military operations. Result: terrorism increased (TTP), billions in losses, social problems. Operations: Zarb-e-Azb, Radd-ul-Fasaad.
Afghan War — Millions of Afghan refugees increased economic burden; drug trafficking spread; drone attacks violated sovereignty.
Post-2021 — Taliban took Afghanistan; increased border insecurity and cross-border attacks on Pakistan.

Conclusion

The question is not whether to create new provinces, but how. If handled responsibly — with consensus, constitutional process, and economic planning — new provinces can improve governance and satisfy legitimate grievances, strengthening the federation. Without these conditions, they risk fragmenting it further.
